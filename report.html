Final project report

GrantFit

Funding strategy and proposal readiness for small and midsize nonprofits

In 4.5 years of nonprofit work, I saw funding discovery and evaluation compete with program delivery for the same limited staff time. I built GrantFit to help small and midsize nonprofits decide which opportunities are worth that time before writing begins. It checks hard eligibility rules against quoted funder text, keeps unknowns visible, assesses fit and proposal readiness, and turns the result into a prioritized, evidence-linked recommendation.

1Motivation and problem

I have worked in the nonprofit sector for 4.5 years, and I have seen securing funding become a substantial operational burden. Staff must find relevant opportunities, diversify funding sources, and compare funders' eligibility rules, restrictions and application requirements. Then they must judge which opportunities and proposal concepts are worth an application, all while delivering existing programs.

The causal logic is straightforward. Discovering and evaluating funding takes considerable staff bandwidth, and that bandwidth comes from the people who run ongoing projects, so funding work competes directly with implementation. The obvious remedy, hiring more fundraising staff, is often not available: many nonprofits operate under funder-directed budgets, restricted funding arrangements, and staffing or HR quotas that can constrain their ability to add capacity. Better prioritization before proposal writing begins could therefore be valuable: an application declined early returns hours to programs.

That is why I chose to build GrantFit around a decision that I believe deserves more attention in the nonprofit funding process: not just finding funding opportunities, but deciding which ones are worth pursuing. My aim is to help small and midsize nonprofits make better-informed funding decisions before committing scarce staff time to writing. Discovering a potentially relevant grant is only the first step; organizations also need to understand eligibility requirements, assess how well their project fits, and identify what needs strengthening before investing time in a proposal. I wanted to explore how AI agents, combined with explicit rules and source-based evidence, could help structure that decision while making uncertainty visible rather than hiding it behind a score.

Finding a grant is not the same as deciding to pursue it

Grant search tools, funder databases and general chat assistants are good at producing candidate opportunities. The harder question for a small organization comes afterwards: of these, which ones should we actually spend time on? A keyword match does not establish four things.

  • Eligibility. A single sentence such as a budget cap, a geographic limit or "by invitation only" can rule an applicant out, however good the fit looks.
  • Evidence. Requirements should come from the funder's own text, not from a model's paraphrase or memory.
  • Readiness. Even an eligible organization may lack measurable outcomes, a realistic budget or evidence for its claims.
  • Effort. A complex application due soon may not be feasible for a team with a few spare hours a month.

Who I designed for and what decision it supports

I designed GrantFit for one person at a small or midsize nonprofit: an executive director, development lead or program manager who handles funding among other duties. That person has limited staff time, often faces eligibility uncertainty in loosely written guidelines, and needs to judge whether the proposal concept is ready before writing. The decision GrantFit supports is narrow on purpose: for one organization, one opportunity and one proposal concept, it recommends one of six tiers (pursue now, strengthen then pursue, verify first, cultivate a relationship, deprioritize, or not eligible) and says why. A seventh outcome, incomplete assessment, appears when there is too little information to rate. It also says what to do next and what a human must check.

What GrantFit does not do matters as much. It does not estimate the probability of winning. It does not invent funder history or eligibility rules. It does not write the proposal. These limits are enforced in code and tests (Section 5), not just stated in a prompt.

2What is new here

I did not build a better grant database. Established products have far larger funder datasets and complete workflows. My contribution is a decision layer that sits between "here is an opportunity" and "start writing". It rests on a few design commitments that I implemented and tested.

  1. Prioritization plus readiness, not just matching. Fit, concept quality, effort and deadline feasibility feed one rule-ordered recommendation, together with a prioritized list of next steps.
  2. Provenance for every requirement. A requirement taken from guideline text is used only if its exact quote is found in that text, and only if the extracted value appears inside that quote. Discovery cards label each field as an official listing field, quoted from the page, or no source evidence.
  3. Unknowns stay unknown. Missing facts produce UNKNOWN and a verify first recommendation. An unverified rule can never cause a FAIL.
  4. Deterministic hard gates. Eligibility, tier ordering, effort and deadline checks are fixed code. A high model score cannot override a failed eligibility rule.
  5. Model judgment where judgment is needed. gpt-6-luna extracts candidate requirements and rates 11 fit and concept dimensions, with a rationale and the input fields it relied on. It can say "insufficient information" instead of guessing.
  6. Safety by construction. A prompt-injection scan, sentence withholding, an output validator and award-probability redaction all run. Human-review items are kept separate from advisories.
  7. Per-call telemetry. Every run writes input, cached-input and output tokens, latency and gateway cache status to a JSONL trace.

3The system I built

GrantFit is a Python 3 application: about 3,700 lines in the core package (grantfit/), 2,100 in the Dash web interface (app/) and 2,300 in tests. It has a web interface and a command-line interface. Every model call goes to gpt-6-luna through the Portkey gateway using the OpenAI Responses API. Implemented Tested

Workflow

  1. Discover (optional). Search Grants.gov (public API, no key, no model call) [4], opt into a gpt-6-luna web search that keeps only URLs the model actually cites, or add a funder page by URL. Results can be saved to a shortlist in the browser, and any card can be handed to the assessment.
  2. Assess. Fill in three short forms: the nonprofit profile, the opportunity (optionally with pasted or retrieved guidelines), and the proposal concept. Only three fields are required. Blank fields stay blank and are never assumed.
  3. Act. The results show the recommendation, the eligibility checks with the funder's wording, the scores with their coverage, the effort and deadline estimate, missing evidence, risks, prioritized next steps, and a separate list of items a human must review. Run details shows each stage with its tokens and latency.

Missing evidence, risks and unsupported-claim judgments. Grants.gov search2 and fetchOpportunity (no key) [4]. gpt-6-luna web_search (opt-in, expensive). URL fetch (public http(s) only, private and loopback hosts refused, 2 MB cap, timeouts).

I kept hard rules out of the model for one reason. An eligibility error is asymmetric: a false PASS wastes days of writing, and a false FAIL silently drops an opportunity. Code that compares a verified quote with a known fact can be tested exhaustively. A model's reading of the same sentence cannot. The model does what code cannot do well: judge how well a mission aligns with funder priorities, or whether an outcome is measurable.

Missing and contradictory information

  • Missing profile facts (budget, legal status, geography, founding year, audit) give UNKNOWN, never an assumption. With no criteria at all, the result is UNKNOWN, or NOT_APPLICABLE for invitation-only or individual giving.
  • Ambiguous or contradictory requirements (for example two different budget caps) give UNKNOWN and require human review. A contradiction cannot hide a real failure (test_contradiction_cannot_hide_a_failure).
  • Missing assessment information. Under assess_v3 the model marks a dimension "insufficient information". That dimension is excluded and the remaining weights are renormalized. Below 60% weight coverage, no score is produced and the tier is INCOMPLETE_ASSESSMENT.

Scoring and recommendation logic

Each rating r ∈ {1..5} maps to s = (r − 1)/4 × 100. A score is the weighted mean over the assessable dimensions. Fit weights are mission .30, population .20, geography .15, award size .15 and funder priorities .20. Concept weights are problem .15, logic model .20, measurable outcomes .20, feasibility .20, budget .15 and evidence .10. The tier is the first matching rule in this order:

  1. Eligibility FAIL → Not eligible
  2. Deadline passed → Deprioritize
  3. Eligibility or opportunity type unknown → Verify first
  4. Model failed or coverage < 60% → Incomplete
  5. Invitation-only or individual giving → Cultivate relationship (or Deprioritize if fit < 50)
  6. Deadline infeasible for capacity → Deprioritize
  7. Fit < 50 → Deprioritize
  8. Concept < 60 → Strengthen then pursue
  9. Request outside stated award range → Strengthen then pursue
  10. Otherwise → Pursue now

The thresholds (50, 60, 60% coverage), the weights and the effort hours are my design choices. They are labeled as such in config/scoring.yaml and have not been calibrated against real award decisions. Appendix A gives the full definitions.

Worked example: an ambiguous geographic rule (recorded live output, synthetic case)

Provenance. The following is the genuine gpt-6-luna output saved during evaluation run 20261026T025208Z for case E16. It was not re-run for this report. The organization and funder are fictional: I wrote them to test ambiguity. It shows how the system behaves, not a real user's outcome.

Input. The fictional "Elm City Reading Partners" serves New Haven and West Haven, CT, and requests $60,000. The pasted guidelines from the fictional "Riverbend Giving Circle" say: "Organizations should generally be based in New England or have strong ties to the region." and "Grants of up to $40,000 are available."

What happened, stage by stage. Extraction proposed the sentence as a requirement and flagged it as unclear. Quote verification confirmed the exact quote. The eligibility rules would not treat "should generally… or strong ties" as a hard geography rule, so the check is UNKNOWN, and overall eligibility is UNKNOWN. Rule 3 then gives Verify first. The tier reasons also record that the $60,000 request is outside the stated range ("?–$40,000"; the minimum is unknown). The next steps, which are ordered by rules, start with a Blocker: "Confirm this eligibility requirement before investing writing time". After that come clarifying the requirement with the funder and softening the claim "proven to double reading growth", which the deterministic check found unsupported by the listed evidence. The run made 2 calls: 3,062 input tokens (1,347 cached), 2,744 output tokens, 26.1 s of stage latency, unpriced.

My assessment of this output. The answer key accepts UNKNOWN or PASS. A human reader would probably decide that a Connecticut organization satisfies the rule, so GrantFit is more cautious here than necessary. I chose that trade-off on purpose: a one-email verification is cheap, and a silent false PASS is expensive. The cost is that GrantFit can feel over-cautious on loosely worded rules.

Cost formula, applied only once verified rates are entered in config/pricing.yaml: cost = (input − cached) × rinput + cached × rcached + output × routput. Output already includes reasoning tokens. The code implements exactly this, and records cost_source: "unpriced" when rates are null.

What the numbers say. (1) One web search costs about four times the input tokens of four complete assessments, so I made it opt-in and made Grants.gov the default. (2) Assessments are output-heavy: output roughly equals input, and about 38% of assessment output is reasoning. Shorter rationales would save more than shorter prompts. (3) GrantFit used 2.25× the baseline's tokens and 2.4× its latency for the same 17 cases (Section 6). That is the price of a separate extraction call and longer, structured rationales.

6Evaluation and results

Method

  • Dataset. I wrote 17 fictional cases (eval/cases/E01–E17, generated by eval/build_cases.py). They cover clear eligibility (2), hard failures (4), missing information (1), relationship funding (2), weak concepts (2), an infeasible deadline (1), prompt injection (2), unsupported claims (1), contradiction (1) and ambiguity (1). Each case has an answer key that accepts a set of tiers and eligibility statuses. Ambiguous cases accept more than one answer.
  • Baseline. The same model, the same inputs and the same output vocabulary, but a single prompt with no tools, quote verification or validators (baseline_v1). It is a fair stand-in for "ask a capable LLM".
  • Runs. Run 2 (eval/results/20261026T025208Z) is the reported run: live, --fresh (gateway cache bypassed), 0 cache replays, 0 degraded stages, 127.4 s wall clock. Run 1 (20261026T024807Z) is reported for comparison. The prompts were extract_v1 and assess_v2; the current assess_v3 postdates both runs.
  • Scoring. Automatic, in eval/metrics.py. "Tier acceptable" means the tier is in the case's allowed set.

What the results show

  • Baseline misses on tier. E02: an evidence-backed concept was under-rated ("strengthen" instead of "pursue now"). E11: an infeasible deadline was not deprioritized; the baseline has no effort-versus-capacity check. E17: a geography failure stated in pasted guidelines was rated UNKNOWN / Verify first instead of FAIL. In run 1, the baseline gave E17 a false PASS.
  • Where GrantFit did not beat the baseline: on 7 of 13 metrics the two systems tied (one more does not apply to the baseline). That includes no false FAILs, no "apply" advice when forbidden, and no probability language. A strong single prompt already handles the easy cases.
  • Run 1 versus run 2. In run 1 GrantFit scored 16/17 on tier: the Azure content filter refused E13's injected guideline text, and the stage degraded safely. I then redacted flagged spans before extraction. Run 2 scored 17/17, but it is not a held-out result: I made the fix after seeing that case.
  • Score stability. The same input produced concept scores of 61.2 and 57.5 on two live runs. That is enough to cross the 60 threshold, so tier boundaries near a cut-off are sensitive to model variance.
  • Human review. I replayed the run-2 outputs through my audited logic with no model calls. Required review dropped from 17/17 to 15/17 cases, and all other metrics were unchanged.

7Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • No real-world validation. No nonprofit staff have used GrantFit. I have no evidence about time saved, decision quality or funding outcomes.
  • Small, self-authored evaluation (n = 17), and run 2 is not held out.
  • Uncalibrated thresholds and weights. They are design choices, not fitted to award decisions.
  • Model variance can move a score across a threshold (61.2 vs 57.5).
  • Narrow reading. Plain sentences only: PDFs, tables and linked documents are not interpreted.
  • Narrow discovery. Grants.gov covers federal listings. Web search is costly and returns few cited pages.
  • Effort affects the tier only through deadline feasibility. There is no "too much effort for the award size" rule.
  • Over-caution on loosely worded rules (E16).
  • Cost is unpriced until verified rates are entered.

Next steps

  1. Validate with practitioners. Collect real guideline documents and staff decisions, then measure agreement on eligibility and tier. Only then calibrate the thresholds.
  2. Build a held-out, larger test set, including adversarial guideline text written by someone other than me. Rerun with assess_v3 and report variance across repeated runs.
  3. Read PDFs and tables with the same quote-verification guarantee. Add a justified effort-to-award-value rule once real effort data exist.
  4. Enter verified prices and report the cost per assessment.

8Conclusion

I started from a problem I saw firsthand in nonprofit work: deciding which funding opportunities deserve scarce staff time competes with delivering programs, and many organizations cannot simply hire their way out of it. I built GrantFit to address that step after search: deciding whether an opportunity deserves a small team's writing time. I separated what code can decide reliably (eligibility gates, quote verification, rule order, effort and deadline arithmetic) from what needs model judgment (fit, readiness and missing evidence). Then I made the boundary visible in the interface and in the traces. On 17 synthetic cases, this structure produced acceptable recommendations in 17 cases, against 14 for a strong single-prompt baseline. It caught 5 of 5 hard eligibility failures against 4 of 5, at about 2.25 times the tokens and 2.4 times the latency.

The most useful lessons for me were about failure. A substring bug ("Kansas" inside "Arkansas") produced a confident false PASS. Telling the model to "score conservatively" quietly turned missing information into low scores. A human-review flag that fired on every case meant nothing. Each was fixed only because I audited the code path instead of the documentation. What I can claim is that GrantFit's mechanisms work as designed on the cases I wrote. My experience tells me this decision is worth supporting; whether GrantFit actually helps real nonprofits make it better, or returns any time to their programs, is the question I have not yet answered.
